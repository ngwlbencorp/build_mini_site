Product Writeup Guideline
0. The Basics
Product Name: SCOPE (Surfacing Capability Overlap and Portfolio Evidence)
Product One-liner (< 150 words)
Capability overlap across whole-of-government (WOG) products carries significant cost at WOG scale – directionally, S$165M – S$330M annually as an illustrative range – and the actors best placed to detect it lack systematic visibility. Cheaper to build doesn’t mean cheaper to run, secure or sustain.
SCOPE is a decision-support tool that surfaces capability overlaps against existing WOG products. From a simple query to a full proposal, SCOPE returns existing product(s) and capabilities that could meet stated needs. The output is evidence, not a verdict; users retain full discretion to act on it.
Hackathon scope vs longer-term vision.
SCOPE is designed as a three-stage system. The {build} 2026 prototype validates Stage 0 (officer-facing discovery for PMs, BAs, and product managers searching the WOG portfolio), tested through a technical experiment described in Section 3. Stage 1 (Years 1–2) extends the same engine to funding gates with voluntary use and concrete incentives for proposers who engage. Stage 2 (Year 2+) makes use mandatory for scale-stage proposals where overlap cost is highest. The technical core is identical across all three stages; what differs is the consumer and the rollout posture. We chose this staging because Stage 0 tests the highest-risk technical assumption directly and produces the validated artefact that earns the conversation for Stages 1 and 2 (full detail in Section 4).

1. Problem Statement
Users: Who are the people affected? Include the size/quantity of users affected if possible.
Primary (longer-term deployment): Approving authorities at central funding and approval committees (IB) and agency-level equivalents, who evaluate proposals seeking additional resources to take digital products into production and/or scale them.
Secondary (and {build} test cohort): Officers across WOG who currently search for or evaluate existing WOG products against new use cases when scoping or writing proposals – PMs, BAs, product managers, delivery managers and any non-GovTech officer looking for existing solutions. We estimate this in the hundreds across WOG.
Use Case: What are they trying to do and what task are they trying to complete? Where and when do they encounter this problem?
Primary (longer-term): When a proposal lands at a funding or approval committee, approvers need to evaluate whether the proposed capability already exists, in whole or in part, somewhere in the WOG product portfolio – but lack systematic means to do so.
Secondary ({build} test cohort): Officers writing or scoping new proposals need to identify whether existing WOG products can meet their stated needs. They encounter this when shaping a problem statement, evaluating build-vs-reuse, or preparing a proposal for funding.
Pain Point: What is the possible root cause behind the problem you have observed?
Approving authorities and proposal authors have no systematic way to detect capability overlap. Detection relies on the institutional memory of the assessor or proposer, the proposer’s own framing of related work, and ad-hoc cross-checks. Our producer-side poll (Section 2) confirms the gap is structural, not a marketing failure of any individual product. We expect it to compound as AI-assisted development continues to lower the cost of building from scratch, weakening the natural friction that previously slowed redundant builds.
Consequence: What happens if we don’t solve the problem? Size your consequence based on the magnitude of effect on your users.
At WOG scale the avoidable cost is material. As a directional illustration: 5–10% of Singapore’s FY24 ICT spend (S$3.3B[3]) going toward overlapping capabilities is S$165M – S$330M annually (methodology in Annex A). Without intervention, WOG continues to accumulate these costs across maintenance, security, compliance, and the dilution of investment that should have gone into fewer, more mature products. Government precedents (US GAO; UK State of Digital Government Review)[1][2] show fragmentation, overlap, and duplication are documented and costly across comparable governments.
The problem will not resolve itself: government products cannot be silently culled by market forces, and by the time consolidation is politically necessary, products are typically embedded in operations and citizen-facing flows. Annex B has the full argument.

3. Solutioning
Problem-Solution Fit: How does your product specifically address the pain points identified?
SCOPE searches the WOG product catalogue at the level of capability (not product name or owning agency) and returns matches and combinations relevant to the stated need. It does not change decision rights; it changes the information available to whoever is making the decision. For the {build} cohort, this is a faster path to a more complete answer when scoping a proposal. For the longer-term cohort (approving authorities), the same engine surfaces the build-vs-reuse question at the gate where it matters most.
Why a gate, not a portal. Three alternative interventions exist: upstream awareness/discoverability portals, composability tooling for builders, and mandatory pre-submission overlap declarations. None reaches every proposal at the moment resources are committed; all rely on the proposer choosing to engage or being able to declare overlap they do not know exists. The funding gate is the one point that satisfies all three: it reaches every proposal, the actor has authority, and intervention occurs before resources are committed.
Functionality: What are your key user flows/features?
•	User uploads a proposal document or system description or asks a free-form question.
•	SCOPE parses the input into capability claims (function, user/data scope, interface).
•	It searches the indexed WOG product catalogue and returns a list of overlapping or partially-overlapping products with matched capability dimensions.
Technical approach. Capability extraction parses proposal text and product documentation into structured representations. Matching combines retrieval over the indexed catalogue with capability-level scoring rather than name-level keyword search, surfacing direct and partial matches. Under the hood, an AI Agent has access to tools to perform keyword search, semantic search and hybrid search to find products that can address the capabilities that the proposal wishes to build. The AI Agent will compare what existing products can do against what the proposed new product seeks to implement, to assess the degree of overlap between existing products and the proposed new product.
User Experience: How did you make the experience seamless/intuitive for the end user?
SCOPE accepts free-form input – a proposal, a system description, or a natural-language question – and returns an evidence-linked list. No new interface to learn beyond “describe what you’re trying to do”.
User Validation: What feedback did users give about the product? What worked well and what required improvement?
User feedback is being captured through the technical experiment described below.
Technical experiment.
What we’re testing and why. The dead-on-arrival risk for SCOPE is whether the matching engine returns relevant overlaps when given real WOG inputs. If it does, the thesis survives even on partial coverage. If returns are inaccurate/unreliable, no rollout strategy saves the proposal. The experiment tests this directly.
Design. Ten users provide their own prompts, questions, or documents – representing real queries officers have when scoping or evaluating a proposal. This are then input into SCOPE and returns the output to the originating user for review. Output is independently reviewed by product managers of the products SCOPE surfaces, providing an accuracy check. PSO and TMO officers may comment as proxy-business-owner perspectives. Approving authorities are the longer-term target but recruiting them now would conflate technical validation with adoption signal, and approaching them with a working, validated prototype is structurally more impactful than approaching them earlier with a concept.
4. Impact & Next Steps
Success Metrics: What is your “North Star” metric?
Decision shifts at the funding gate attributable to overlap evidence – the proportion of committee reviews in which the overlap evidence demonstrably influenced the decision (reuse outcome, scope change, withdrawal, or documented justification to proceed despite overlap).
Leading indicators: coverage (% of in-scope proposals run through SCOPE before committee review); quality of evidence (precision and recall of overlap detection on a labelled set, calibrated post-experiment); stickiness (% of officers who return to SCOPE more than once per quarter, target 50%+); and time-to-evidence (median time from proposal submission to SCOPE output, as an operational signal of tool performance).
Lagging indicators: SCOPE citation rate in committee papers (Stage 1+, % of committee papers that cite SCOPE output as a factor in the decision, measurable from the committee secretariat’s existing paper trail); documented avoided-cost cases (Stage 1+, cumulative count and value of cases where SCOPE output was the deciding factor in a reuse outcome, with documented avoided development and maintenance cost); and user-reported usefulness (% of users who report SCOPE changed what they did, captured via a one-question post-use follow-up).
Three-stage system. SCOPE is a three-stage system; the {build} 2026 prototype validates Stage 0. Stage 0 (now): officer-facing discovery for PMs, BAs, and product managers searching the WOG portfolio – validated through the technical experiment in Section 3. Stage 1 (Years 1–2): voluntary use at funding gates with concrete incentives for proposers who engage (e.g. expedited clearance) regardless of overlap findings – building the run-case corpus and establishing expected-but-not-mandatory practice. Stage 2 (Year 2+): mandatory at scale-stage proposals (e.g. CDB/PSIRC) where overlap cost is highest. The technical core is identical across all three stages; what differs is the consumer and the rollout posture.
Expected Impact: What outcomes do you expect this to be able to deliver in the short/medium/long term if your product is launched to users?
Short term (0–6 months post-hackathon, Stage 0 → Stage 1 transition): Working prototype with measured matching performance on real WOG inputs (from {build} experiment). Sponsor identification and pilot deployment with one approving authority/committee, beginning Stage 1.
Medium term (6–18 months, Stage 1 in operation): Voluntary use at funding gates is normalised across multiple agencies’ funding/approval committees, with concrete incentives drawing engagement. The overlap engine functions across multiple surfaces – an officer-facing search interface and a developer-facing API – with a feedback loop to PSO and PMP on systemic gaps.
Long term (18+ months, Stage 2): Mandatory use at scale-stage proposals (e.g. CDB/PSIRC). Capability overlap becomes a routine input to funding decisions across WOG. SCOPE’s run-case corpus produces the first systematic measurement of WOG capability overlap cost, replacing the illustrative estimate in this writeup with a measured figure.
Operational dependencies. Two things must happen for the staged rollout to materialise: (i) a sponsor with portfolio-level authority is secured – the validated prototype is the artefact that earns this conversation, with active engagements summarised in Section 2 (Stakeholder Buy-in); (ii) Stage 1’s incentive structure is endorsed by at least one funding committee. Detection reliability is addressed by tool design (output surfaces evidence and links to underlying matches, never asserts a verdict), with Stage 1 doubling as a calibration period before any stricter posture is applied.

Annex A. Cost Estimation Methodology
Singapore illustrative estimate: S$165M – S$330M annually. Singapore Government FY24 ICT spend is approximately S$3.3 billion[3]. The range above represents what 5–10% of this spend going toward capabilities that overlap with what already exists would amount to. The figure is illustrative, not measured.
Method and caveats. We do not have a Singapore-specific benchmark for capability overlap as a percentage of ICT spend; the 5–10% range is therefore a working assumption rather than a derived estimate. The range is intended to convey order of magnitude, not precision. We treat 5% as a conservative working assumption and 10% as a plausible ceiling pending direct measurement; readers should apply their own judgement on whether this range is plausible for the WOG context.
Government precedents support the order of magnitude. The US GAO has documented approximately US$725 billion in financial benefits from 2011 to 2025 by addressing fragmentation, overlap, and duplication across the federal government[1]; a single recommendation in their 2025 annual report on IT portfolio reviews was assessed as capable of generating US$100M+ on its own. The UK’s 2025 State of Digital Government Review identified fragmentation and duplication as systemic (44 different ways to prove identity to government in 2021), with broader digitisation savings estimated at £45 billion per year, or 4–7% of total UK public sector spend[2]. These are not direct analogues for capability overlap in WOG ICT spend specifically, but they show that the structural conditions producing material avoidable costs are well-documented in comparable governments.
The absence of any systematic measurement of capability overlap cost in Singapore is itself part of the problem SCOPE addresses. A medium-term outcome of SCOPE adoption is the data needed to replace this estimate with a measured figure.
Annex B. Why a Wait-and-See Approach Fails
A natural objection is that the problem may resolve itself – that market dynamics or future rationalisation exercises will eventually consolidate overlapping capabilities. This thinking does not hold for government products.
First, government products cannot be silently culled by market forces. Each represents committed taxpayer spend with public-justification requirements; sunsetting a product is itself a multi-stakeholder exercise.
Second, by the time rationalisation is politically necessary, products are typically embedded in public service operations and citizen-facing flows, meaning the rationalisation itself imposes downstream cost on operations and on members of the public. The UK experience documents this trajectory: the 2025 State of Digital Government Review found public sector technology had become fragmented to the point that consolidation now requires sustained, multi-year reform programmes; even where top-down consolidation has succeeded (such as GOV.UK replacing more than 1,800 separate websites), it has been limited by lack of sustained senior sponsorship, uneven funding, and constrained scope[3].
Third, even within structures designed for alignment, consolidation is hard. One PM in our poll, working within a Programme structure where overlapping products share ownership and incentives, reported that “resolving capability overlaps between products still takes time and effort”. Outside such structures, the friction is materially higher.
The cheapest moment to prevent overlap is before resources are committed. Every year of unfettered creation compounds both the cost of consolidation and the operational risk of eventual culling.
